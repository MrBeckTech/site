IN PLAIN WORDS

Mass means how much stuff something has. We measure it in grams (g).

In a reaction, atoms are taken apart and put back together in new ways, like LEGO bricks. You can't lose LEGO bricks, and no new ones appear.

So the mass at the end is the same as the mass at the start. This is the law of conservation of mass.

BUT MY SCALE CHANGED!

If the scale says it got lighter, some bricks (a gas) floated away. If it got heavier, some bricks (a gas from the air) joined in. The mass didn't vanish or appear. It just moved on or off the scale.

HOW TO DO IT
  1. Find the side where you know every mass. Add those masses up.
  2. Mass is conserved, so the other side has the same total.
  3. Subtract the masses you know. What is left is the missing mass.

THE LAW

Law of conservation of mass: in a chemical reaction, the total mass of the reactants (what you start with) equals the total mass of the products (what you make).

Atoms are not made or destroyed. They are only rearranged. Same atoms, same mass.

If a scale shows a change, a gas got in or out. Use a sealed (closed) container and the mass stays the same.

BIG QUESTIONS

A nail rusts. Why does it get heavier?

Rust forms when iron joins with oxygen from the air (and some water). The rust holds the iron and the oxygen, so the nail weighs more. The extra mass is the oxygen that joined in.

Wood burns. Why is the ash so much lighter?

Most of the atoms in wood leave as gases: carbon dioxide and water vapour. Only the ash stays behind. If you could catch every gas (and count the oxygen the fire used), the total mass would be the same as before.

Steel wool burns. Why does it get heavier?

It is the same idea as rusting, just much faster. Oxygen from the air joins the iron, so the solid left behind is heavier.
