PART 2: CONCEPTS & REVIEW QUESTIONS

1. Earth's Energy Budget & Global Thermal Balance

  • Incoming Solar Radiation: ~50% passes through the atmosphere and is absorbed by land/ocean. The remaining 50% is absorbed by atmospheric gases/particles (20%), reflected by clouds/dust (25%), or reflected by Earth's surfaces (5%).
  • Outward Radiation Balance: To maintain stable global temperatures, Earth radiates an equivalent amount of thermal energy back into space as long-wavelength infrared radiation.

2. Albedo & The Hydrosphere as a Heat Sink

  • Albedo Dynamics: Light surfaces (snow/ice) have high albedo (reflect 70–80%). Dark surfaces (soil/ocean) have low albedo, absorbing most solar energy.
  • Hydrosphere Heat Sink: Water's high specific heat capacity allows it to absorb vast thermal energy without drastic temperature shifts, buffering global climate.

3. Impact of Specific Heat Capacity on Air Temp

  • Terrestrial Heating: Land heats up and cools down rapidly due to low specific heat capacity, leading to extreme daily and seasonal fluctuations.
  • Aquatic Moderation: Water warms and cools gradually, creating milder winters and cooler summers in coastal regions.

4. Thermal Energy Transfer Mechanisms

  • Radiation: Solar shortwave radiation warms the surface, which re-emits longwave thermal infrared radiation.
  • Conduction: Heat transfers through particle-to-particle collisions when air contacts warm ground or water.
  • Convection: Warm air expands, rises, and is replaced by cooler, denser air, forming vertical atmospheric circulation cells.

5. Greenhouse Effect as an Atmospheric Heat Sink

  • Mechanisms: Greenhouse gases (H₂O vapor, CO₂, CH₄) absorb emitted longwave IR radiation and re-radiate thermal energy in all directions.
  • Climate Role: Traps energy in the troposphere to maintain average global temperatures suitable for life.

6. Phase Changes: Endothermic vs. Exothermic

  • Heat Absorbed (Endothermic): Melting, Evaporation, and Sublimation require water molecules to absorb energy from their surroundings.
  • Heat Released (Exothermic): Freezing, Condensation, and Deposition release stored latent thermal energy into ambient air/water.

7. Latent Heat of Fusion & Vaporization

  • Latent Heat of Fusion: Energy absorbed or released during transitions between solid and liquid states at constant temperature.
  • Latent Heat of Vaporization: Energy absorbed or released during transitions between liquid and gas states at constant temperature.

8. Heat Transfer in the Global Water Cycle

  • Evaporative Storage: Solar energy converts sensible heat into latent heat stored within water vapor.
  • Atmospheric Release: Condensation releases latent heat high in the troposphere as clouds form, fueling storms.

9. Convection vs. Advection

  • Convection: Vertical transfer of energy driven by buoyancy and thermal density differences.
  • Advection: Horizontal transfer of energy or mass by moving fluids (e.g., winds or surface currents).

10. Solar Insolation & Spherical Geometry

  • Equatorial Direct Rays: Rays strike directly at 90°, concentrating energy over a minimal surface area.
  • Polar Slanted Rays: Solar energy strikes at oblique angles, spreading energy over a larger surface area with lower absorption.

11. Pressure, Air Density, & Wind Generation

  • Low Pressure: Heated air expands, becomes less dense, and ascends.
  • High Pressure: Cooling air contracts, becomes denser, and sinks.
  • Wind Generation: Pressure gradients force air to move horizontally from high pressure to low pressure.

12. Air Masses: Classification & Formation

  • Arctic (A): Bitterly cold and dry.
  • Continental Polar (cP): Cold and dry.
  • Maritime Polar (mP): Cool and humid.
  • Continental Tropical (cT): Hot and dry.
  • Maritime Tropical (mT): Warm and humid.

13. High vs. Low Pressure Systems

  • High Pressure (Anticyclone): Sinking, dense, dry air flowing outward. Clockwise rotation in NH; produces clear, fair weather.
  • Low Pressure (Cyclone): Rising warm air drawing air inward. Counterclockwise rotation in NH; generates clouds and precipitation.

14. Global Circulation & Coriolis Effect

  • Global Convection: Equatorial heating and polar cooling drive massive atmospheric circulation cells.
  • Coriolis Deflection: Earth's rotation deflects air right in the NH and left in the SH, creating 3 distinct circulation belts per hemisphere.

15. Major Planetary Wind Systems

  • Trade Winds: 0°–30° latitude; blow persistently from east to west toward the equator.
  • Prevailing Westerlies: 30°–60° latitude; blow from west to east, steering North American weather.
  • Polar Easterlies: 60° to poles; transport cold arctic air from east to west.

16. Dynamics of the Polar Jet Stream

  • Characteristics: High-speed westerly winds (~7,000–12,000 m altitude) near 40°–60°N latitude.
  • Weather Impact: Thermal boundary separating polar and tropical air; jet stream undulations steer surface storm systems.

17. Atmospheric Fronts

  • Cold Front: Cold air wedges under warm air, creating intense, short duration precipitation/thunderstorms.
  • Warm Front: Warm air gently climbs over cold air, creating overcast stratus clouds and prolonged rain.
  • Stationary Front: Two non-advancing air masses meet, creating extended periods of rain and clouds.
  • Occluded Front: Fast cold front overtakes warm front, lifting warm air off the surface; brings complex clouds and heavy rain.

18. Surface Ocean Currents

  • Mechanism: Driven by prevailing surface winds and deflected by landmasses and Coriolis effect into giant ocean gyres.
  • Heat Transport: Warm currents move tropical energy poleward (west boundaries); cold currents move polar water toward equator (east boundaries).

19. Local Impacts: Gulf Stream & Labrador Current

  • Gulf Stream: Carries warm tropical waters northward along eastern North America, moderating Atlantic Canada's winter temps.
  • Labrador Current: Transports cold Arctic water south, chilling summer coastal temps and creating heavy sea fog when meeting warm air.

20. Thermohaline Circulation

  • Drivers: Driven by water density differences from temperature ('thermo') and salinity ('haline').
  • Global Conveyor Belt: Cold, dense, saline polar water sinks and flows globally before upwelling, circulating thermal energy and nutrients planet-wide.
